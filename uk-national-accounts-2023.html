HOW TO READ THESE TABLES

Each account has two sides. Resources are what comes in to a sector; uses are what goes out. The two must match, and the figure that makes them match — the balancing item, shown in orange — is carried down into the account below. That is how the sequence links together:

GDP → operating surplus → disposable income → saving → net lending
↓
WHAT THIS ACCOUNT SHOWS

What was produced, and what it took to produce it. The resources side lists everything available to the economy: output from each sector, plus taxes less subsidies on products, plus imports. The uses side subtracts intermediate consumption — the goods and services used up in making other goods and services — and what remains is gross domestic product. Exports appear here as a use, because the rest of the world is who buys them. Subtracting the wear and tear on buildings and machinery gives net domestic product.

WHAT THIS ACCOUNT SHOWS

Who earned the value that was just produced. Gross domestic product enters as a resource and is paid out on the uses side: to employees as wages and employers’ social contributions, and to government as taxes on production less subsidies. What is left over is the balancing item — operating surplus, which is broadly profit, and mixed income, the earnings of the self-employed, where wage and profit cannot be separated.

NOTE — ACCOUNTS NOT SHOWN HERE

The next account below opens with gross disposable income already calculated. That figure is not produced out of thin air: it is the result of three accounts sitting between the generation of income account and the use of disposable income account, which have been left out here to keep this page readable. If you want to see how income gets from operating surplus to disposable income, those are the accounts to look up in the Blue Book:

1.7.3 — Allocation of primary income account. Carries operating surplus, mixed income, compensation of employees, taxes less subsidies on production and imports, and property income through to the gross balance of primary incomes, otherwise known as gross national income.

1.7.4 — Secondary distribution of income account. Adds current taxes on income and wealth, social contributions and benefits, and other current transfers, to reach gross disposable income (B.6g) — the figure the next account starts from.

1.7.5 — Redistribution of income in kind account. Adds social transfers in kind (D.63) to reach adjusted gross disposable income (B.7g).

WHAT THIS ACCOUNT SHOWS

What each sector did with the income it ended up with. Disposable income arrives as a resource, after the tax, benefit and transfer stages that are not shown here. The uses side splits it two ways: final consumption expenditure — spending on goods and services that are used up now — and whatever is not spent, which is the balancing item, gross saving.

WHAT THIS ACCOUNT SHOWS

What happened to that saving. The uses side records spending on new physical assets: gross fixed capital formation, which is new buildings, machinery and infrastructure, plus changes in inventories and valuables. If a sector’s saving exceeds what it spent on such assets, it lends the difference to others; if it falls short, it borrows. That balancing item is net lending or net borrowing.

NOTE — THE REMAINING 1.7 ACCOUNTS

The rest of the 1.7 sequence has been left out. Those accounts concern financial transactions and stocks rather than production, income and non-financial capital: the financial account (1.7.8 net acquisition of financial assets; 1.7.8_2 net acquisition of financial liabilities), the other changes in volume of assets account (1.7.9), the revaluation account (1.7.10, nominal holding gains and losses) and the closing financial balance sheet (1.7.11 total financial assets; 1.7.11_2 total financial liabilities).

One difference from the simplified theory: under the System of National Accounts, government spending isn't lumped together in G. Its current spending — wages, day-to-day running costs — is recorded as government final consumption expenditure (part of C+G above). Its investment spending — new hospitals, roads, equipment — is recorded separately, inside gross capital formation (I), in a different table entirely.
