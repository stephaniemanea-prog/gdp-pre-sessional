Measuring the Macroeconomy

The macroeconomy refers to the whole economy — usually at the level of a country — rather than any single market for a single product. It encompasses the product market (the market for all goods and services), the labour market, and the money market.

This lecture will focus on how we measure the macroeconomy.

Governments, firms, and households constantly make decisions — for instance, whether to raise interest rates, how much to invest, whether to hire — based on some picture of macroeconomic performance, or, in lay terms, "how the economy is doing." That picture has to be built from statistics, and statistics require choices about what to count and what to leave out.

Economic performance, or how well an economy is doing, is often judged based on the rate of economic growth.

What is economic growth?

Economic growth is the rate of change of real GDP from one period to the next:

gt = (Yt − Yt−1) / Yt−1 × 100%

where Yt is real GDP (defined in Section 07) in the current period and Yt−1 is real GDP in the previous period.

What is GDP? At its core, GDP is a measure of production — the value of goods and services an economy actually produces in a given period.

QUESTION

Do you think maximising GDP growth is, on its own, a sufficient goal for macroeconomic policy?

Economist Amartya Sen thought economics should be concerned not only with income and wealth, but with using those resources toward significant ends — including long and worthwhile lives; judging a nation's success by income alone, he argued, risks missing the deeper goal of well-being. What you choose to measure depends on what you think matters economically. This page first introduces the statistical and accounting standards behind GDP and related measures. It then moves on to discuss the critiques of GDP as a measure of economic performance.

02

Who's in "the economy"?

National accounts sort every transaction into one of five institutional sectors, following the internationally agreed System of National Accounts (SNA).

Households

People and unincorporated household businesses — including a family-run market stall.

Non-financial corporations

Firms producing goods or non-financial services for sale — a car manufacturer, a supermarket.

Financial corporations

Banks, insurers, and others whose business is financial intermediation.

Government

Central, regional and local government, plus social security funds.

Rest of the world

Every transaction with someone outside the country's borders.

Every pound, dollar or euro that changes hands in the economy is, in principle, traceable to a transaction between two of these sectors. GDP is what you get when you add up a particular slice of those transactions — the ones that represent newly produced output.

03

What counts as GDP?

Gross Domestic Product is the total market value of final goods and services newly produced within a country's borders over a given period — usually a quarter or a year.

Two words are doing a lot of work here. Final excludes intermediate goods, because counting both the steel and the car built from it would double-count the steel's value. Newly produced excludes anything that merely changes hands — buying a second-hand car doesn't add to this year's GDP, because the car was already counted in the year it was made.

Where GDP draws its outer edge — the production boundary — has shifted a great deal over time, and it's worth knowing roughly where it started.

A short history of "productive activity"

In An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776), Adam Smith drew the line narrowly: only labour that resulted in something tangible and durable — a coat, a plough, a bolt of cloth — counted as productive. The labour of a menial servant, a physician, or an actor left nothing behind once the work was done, however useful or honourable it was, so Smith classed it as unproductive. National accounting has spent almost two and a half centuries pushing that line steadily outward. Today's SNA counts all market services as production — and, as the next question shows, it now counts some things Smith would never have recognised as production at all.

Yes, in principle. The SNA's reasoning is that the production boundary is defined by the activity, not by its legal status: if something is produced and there is a willing buyer and seller, it's a market transaction, whatever the state's view of it.

In plain terms: a farm growing a prohibited crop is still growing something, selling it in a real market, and — usually — employing people to do it, even informally. Excluding it wouldn't make the activity disappear; it would just make the statistics blind to a slice of real economic activity, and inconsistent between countries where the same activity happens to be legal or banned.

The interest flow itself: no. Something built out of it: yes — and this is where the production boundary gets genuinely strange.

In principle, interest is just a transfer of funds from one unit to another, like a tax or a gift — it isn't a payment for a newly produced good or service, so it sits outside GDP. But banks and other financial intermediaries do more than move money around: they assess borrowers, manage liquidity, and absorb risk. The SNA imputes a value for this implicit service, called FISIM: the spread between the rate a bank charges borrowers (or pays savers) and a reference "pure" interest rate is treated as the price of that service, and that spread — not the interest itself — is counted as production and added into GDP.

ONS estimates put FISIM at roughly 2% of nominal UK GDP in a typical year. And because it's calculated from an interest-rate spread, it moves with interest rates themselves, not simply with how much credit is flowing through the economy. FISIM's share of GDP nearly doubled between 2021 and 2023 — while the volume of lending barely moved (UK bank lending grew just 3% over the period; lending to small businesses actually fell). The entire rise, then, comes from banks charging more on loans than they passed on to savers — a wider spread, not more lending. Is that the kind of value Adam Smith would call productive — or is it market power, perhaps even implicit coordination across a concentrated banking sector, letting banks extract a bigger cut, simply relabelled by the accounts as output?

Own-account goods: yes. If a household grows vegetables, sews clothing, or builds something for its own use rather than for sale, the SNA still counts it as production and imputes a value for it. Excluding it would understate GDP heavily wherever subsistence production is common — and it would make GDP artificially higher in a country where the identical food is grown commercially and sold, purely because of how the transaction is organised, not because more was actually produced.

Owner-occupied housing: also yes, via an imputation. If you own the home you live in, you don't pay yourself rent — but the SNA treats you as though you did. Statisticians estimate what your home would rent for on the open market, and add that hypothetical rent to GDP, as if you were a landlord "selling" housing services to yourself as a tenant. Without this imputation, GDP would look smaller in countries with high home ownership than in otherwise identical countries where people mostly rent — a difference in who holds the title deed, not in how people actually live. Own-account services: no. Unpaid cooking, cleaning, and childcare inside the home sit outside the boundary entirely — even though they are economically indistinguishable, in terms of the labour involved, from the identical task performed by someone you pay. Hire a nanny for the same childcare, and it enters GDP the moment money changes hands. This is the line that draws the sharpest objection of the whole boundary — particularly from feminist economics — and you'll meet it again in Section 08.

04

Three ways to arrive at the same number

Every pound of output is, at the same time, a pound of someone's income and a pound of someone's spending. So GDP can be measured three ways — and, done correctly, all three must land on an identical total.

ApproachWhat it adds up
ProductionThe value added by every producer (sales minus the cost of intermediate inputs)
IncomeEveryone's earnings from producing that output — wages (labour income) plus profits (capital income)
ExpenditureWhat was spent buying the final output — consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports

What to notice: however much steel changes hands between the two firms, the production and expenditure totals only ever move with the final sale to households — the intermediate transaction washes out. The income total is the one you can actually break: if wages and profit for a firm don't add up to its value added, the identity fails, because someone's "income" has gone missing. That's not a quirk of this toy economy — it's the accounting identity that ties the three approaches together in every real economy too.

The identity holds because all three are looking at the same flow from different angles. Written out:

The three approaches, as formulas

Production:  GDP = output net of intermediate consumption = the sum of Gross Value Added
Income:  GDP = W + P + MI + (Tp − Sp)
Expenditure:  GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

  • W employee compensation (wages) and employers' social contributions
  • P operating surplus — the return to capital, what's left for owners once labour and inputs are paid
  • MI mixed income — the self-employed, where wage and profit can't be cleanly separated
  • Tp − Sp taxes on production and imports, less subsidies
  • C household consumption
  • I investment in new capital — buildings, machinery, infrastructure, plus additions to inventories
  • G government spending (consumption and investment)
  • X − M exports minus imports

05

GNI — a better measure of living standards?

GDP measures value added produced within a country's borders, regardless of who owns the factors producing it. Gross National Income (GNI) instead measures income earned by a country's residents, wherever in the world it was earned (for example, dividends a resident receives from a foreign-owned company, or wages sent home by a resident working abroad — set against profits foreign owners repatriate out of the country, or wages paid to foreign workers inside it).

The identity

GNI = GDP + income residents earn abroad − income foreigners earn domestically

Because GNI reflects income actually available to residents, it's often considered the better yardstick for living standards — which is why bodies like the World Bank use GNI per capita, not GDP per capita, to classify countries by income level. 06

NDP — a better measure of sustainable economic growth?

You've already met NDP once, in passing, as "GDP minus depreciation." In March 2025, the UN Statistical Commission adopted the 2025 SNA — the first major revision of the international accounting rulebook since 2008 — and one of its central changes is to push NDP much further into the foreground. It's worth pausing on what that actually means before Section 08, because "Net Domestic Product" tends to mean nothing at all until you've seen it worked through.

GDP is gross: it adds up everything newly produced in a year, full stop. It doesn't ask what it cost to keep the economy's productive capacity intact — machinery wearing out, buildings ageing, equipment needing replacement. NDP nets that out. Think of a firm that makes $1m in sales this year but whose machinery loses $150,000 in value through wear and tear: its true addition to the economy's productive capacity is closer to $850,000, not $1m. Run that logic over the whole economy, and GDP minus this wear-and-tear figure — consumption of fixed capital — gives you NDP.

What's new in the 2025 SNA Manual

The 2008 rulebook already defined NDP this way, but treated it as a secondary figure — GDP stayed the single headline number everyone quoted. The 2025 revision does two things differently. First, it recommends NDP be published and discussed alongside GDP as the more informative measure of sustainable economic growth. Second — and this is the part worth sitting with — it now recommends factoring in the depletion of natural resources, not just wear-and-tear on machinery, when calculating NDP. Draw down a stock of oil, minerals, or forest, and that loss is now meant to count as a cost of production, exactly like a machine wearing out. Still, the 2025 depletion adjustment only covers natural resources with a market price, leaving out things like biodiversity loss or ecosystem degradation.

GDP vs NDP, side by side

NDP = GDP − depreciation of fixed capital − (as of 2025) depletion of natural resources

The gap between the two isn't trivial. In the UK, consumption of fixed capital alone ran at about 15% of GDP in 2023 and 2024 (ONS) — before any adjustment for natural resource depletion is even added in. A country can post solid GDP growth while its NDP growth is much weaker, if that growth is coming from running machinery and infrastructure into the ground faster than it's being replaced. NDP is the closer of the two to answering: could this level of output actually be sustained?

07

Nominal vs real GDP

Nominal GDP values output at today's prices — so it rises if firms simply produce more, or if prices simply rise, or both. That makes it a poor way to compare output across years. Real GDP revalues every year's output at the prices of a fixed base year, stripping out the effect of price changes and leaving only the change in quantity produced.

Formally, real GDP is GDP valued at the constant prices of a chosen base year — it is the Yt referenced in Section 01's growth formula.

Non-market production — output that never passes through a market, so is never priced or counted — is large: household labour alone is commonly estimated at 25% to over 50% of GDP.

Between 1973 and 2000 real median family income rose by about 25% in the US — but roughly half of that came from families working around five more weeks a year on average, not from a genuine rise in living standards per hour worked. If households switch from relying on own production (childcare, cooking, cleaning) to sourcing these services from the market, GDP increases, even though the same services were produced in both cases.

Similarly, if households work longer hours and have less leisure time, GDP increases. The value of lost leisure is not recorded anywhere. By the mid-2000s, 44% of US employees described themselves as overworked. Worse still, overworked employees were six times more likely to report stress-related health problems and two-and-a-half times more likely to show symptoms of clinical depression. The impact of overwork on health (one could think of it as "human capital depletion") is not captured in the national accounts.

Spending driven by relative rank rather than absolute need — the technical version of "keeping up with the Joneses." Functional goods hold their value regardless of what others have: your pleasure in good health isn't diminished by your neighbour's good health. Positional goods are bought partly or wholly for the status they signal. When enough people acquire a status good, it loses much of its positional value, pushing everyone toward ever more expensive versions to hold the same relative position — "like the mutually draining expenses of an arms race." GDP counts every round of this spending as full value added, with no way to register that, by construction, not everyone can out-rank everyone else.

People appear to weigh relative standing heavily. This helps explain a genuinely surprising finding: rising average income doesn't reliably raise reported happiness, because what people judge as an acceptable standard of living tracks their income relative to others, not its absolute level. One experiment shows how far this goes: offered $100,000 while everyone else earns $90,000, or $110,000 (a real 10% increase) while everyone else earns $200,000, about half of respondents chose the lower absolute income for the higher relative one.

A related argument, from radical and Marxist economics, is economic waste: spending required not by production itself but by the particular social form production happens to take under competition. The clearest example is the early-1980s US auto industry: for every worker actually building cars, nearly two more were employed supervising production workers or selling the finished cars, and dealership staff alone outnumbered production workers. On the product itself, an estimated 25% of a 1950s car's cost, and 20% of the petrol needed to run it, came from annual style changes, horsepower increases and advertising — spending that wins market share rather than improving the car. On this view, as much as 30% of the economy's inputs may be wasteful in this sense. All of it — the extra supervisors, the retail networks, the marketing and advertising — adds to GDP exactly like any other output, raising the question of whether real value is being created, or whether GDP is mistakenly recording the cost of firms competing for a fixed demand pool.

"Bads" are the negative side-effects of economic activity — pollution, commuting accidents, degraded neighbourhood safety. When something bad happens, the response to it, called a defensive expenditure in economic terms, gets added to GDP as new output, while the bad itself is never subtracted. Cleaning up an oil spill raises GDP; the spill's damage does not lower it. Higher prison or policing spending in response to rising crime counts as more output, even though what's produced is protection from a problem, not a net addition to wellbeing. This might mean that GDP is mistaking an intermediate good (a cost of maintaining a baseline) for a final good (a new benefit).

Rough estimates put "bads" at 5% or more of GDP in a typical year; a 1989 study estimated West Germany's defensive expenditures at over 10% of GNP. On pollution specifically, one estimate put the immediate cost of US air, water, and noise pollution damage in 1990 at around 2% of GDP, with the long-term cost at roughly seven times that.

GDP already nets out one kind of capital consumption — wear and tear on machinery and buildings — to produce Net Domestic Product (NDP). (Before the 2025 SNA Manual) no equivalent deduction is made for natural capital: the stock of oil, minerals, forests, and fisheries an economy draws down as it produces. One estimate put US consumption of non-renewable resources in 1990 alone at around 16% of GDP, plus a further 3% in destroyed farmland and wetlands — costs that appear nowhere in the official figures, because the resources were extracted, not purchased from anyone whose loss gets recorded.

However, a genuine methodological debate arises from this: should the discovery of new reserves count as replenishing natural capital, offsetting depletion? For the US, discoveries and depletions of resources roughly matched between 1958 and 1991, which some economists take as evidence the problem nets out.

Two figures side by side make the point vivid. Between 1973 and 1992, real wages for the bottom 60% of male full-time US workers fell by around 20%. Over the same period, GDP per capita rose by 33%. A single aggregate was climbing while most people's pay packets were falling — and GDP, by design, cannot tell the difference. In the 1980s, all gains in male earnings went to the top 20% of the workforce, with 64% of that going to the top 1% alone, while average CEO pay rose from roughly 35 times to 157 times that of the average production worker.

One proposed fix weights income growth by who receives it, giving extra weight for income reaching the bottom 20% of the distribution, on the grounds of declining marginal utility of money (a given dollar is worth more to a poor household than a rich one). Applying that weighting retrospectively cuts measured US national income growth between 1966 and 1990 by around 20%. The approach rests on a distinction between needs and wants that mainstream economics is often reluctant to make, since it requires comparing utility between people, which neoclassical theory generally treats as unscientific.

Most people spend more time at work, or in activities tied to it — commuting, job-related training — than anywhere except home. Job security, health and safety standards, how interesting the work is, opportunities for advancement, autonomy, and collegiality are all inputs into wellbeing that GDP has no way to register. Two economies could report identical GDP while one runs on secure, autonomous, well-supervised work — and the other on precarious, closely-monitored jobs with high turnover, or even outright forced labour.

Modern examples of migrant labour systems in which workers' passports are confiscated, wages withheld, and freedom of movement restricted have been documented across multiple countries and industries by the ILO and international human rights organisations, sometimes accompanied by high worker death tolls on major construction projects. That output contributes to GDP exactly like any other construction spending. GDP cannot distinguish a building built under decent conditions from one built under conditions amounting to forced labour; a dollar of output is a dollar of output, however it was produced.

In the West, some dimensions of job quality have genuinely improved: better health and safety standards in some sectors (less pesticide exposure for agricultural workers, more climate-controlled offices), wider career access for women, and, perhaps, more flexibility for workers balancing care responsibilities. Others show signs of decline: average job security and on-the-job autonomy appear to have fallen, driven by declining unionisation, rising global competition, and corporate-directed technological change.

Nonetheless, aggregating "quality of work" into a single figure is far harder than aggregating pollution costs or resource depletion — there's no obvious common unit, and reasonable studies reach opposite conclusions on the same question.

A meta-externality is different from an ordinary externality like pollution, which harms an identifiable third party in a specific transaction. It's the effect economic activity has, cumulatively, on background conditions — trust, honesty, willingness to cooperate — that markets themselves depend on to function, but that no single transaction is charged for damaging. Economists call this stock of trust and cooperative capacity social capital: not a fixed endowment, but something built up or run down by how people and firms actually behave.

To see why this might matter, it helps to name something usually left implicit: mainstream economics is built on the assumption that people act out of self-interest. This premise, which goes back to Adam Smith, is formalised into the standard behavioural assumption of microeconomics, and carried through into the representative-agent models used across modern macroeconomics today. Markets are often defended precisely because self-interested behaviour, under the right conditions, produces good collective outcomes without anyone needing to intend them. The meta-externality critique asks a different question: does organising an economy around that assumption, and rewarding people who act on it, change how people actually behave and thus, the very social fabric of a modern capitalist society?

There is a lively academic debate on social capital erosion. The origins of this debate can be found in Putnam (2000), who documents evidence of a decline in trust, organisational membership, volunteering and informal socialising in the US since the 1960s. More recent work (Sarracino, 2012) documents a decline in confidence in institutions (judiciary, religion, parliament, civil service) and interpersonal trust (but not associational membership) in several developed economies.

The incongruence between rising living standards and social capital erosion is not without consequence. If the latter comes as a cost of the former, while it is also an essential precondition for the very functioning of the market system, then, in the words of ecological economist E.F. Schumacher, growth itself can stall — not from any technological failure, but because of "a creeping paralysis of non-cooperation" (Schumacher 1973, p.29, cited in Cohn, 2006, p.83).

Beyond GDP

Alternative measures have been developed to address at least some of the drawbacks of GDP discussed above.

Human Development Index (HDI)

UN index combining income, education, and life expectancy — designed to broaden "development" beyond income alone.

OECD Better Life Index

Compares countries across 11 wellbeing dimensions, from housing to work–life balance, letting users weight them differently.

Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

Measures poverty directly through deprivations — health, education, living standards — rather than income thresholds alone.

Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI)

Starts from GDP, then adjusts for distribution, environmental damage, and unpaid household labour.

Each trades away some of GDP's simplicity and comparability in exchange for capturing something GDP structurally can't. There's no consensus "best" measure — which is itself the point: what you measure depends on what you think matters.

There are several ready-to-use alternatives to GDP, and the case against relying on it alone has come from within the discipline — even from its top. In 2009, the Commission on the Measurement of Economic Performance and Social Progress, chaired by Nobel laureates Joseph Stiglitz and Amartya Sen, concluded that GDP was never designed to measure welfare and shouldn't be treated as if it does: "what we measure affects what we do" (Stiglitz, Sen and Fitoussi, 2009).

GDP persists anyway. It is institutionally codified into national accounting systems and the fiscal rules built on top of them. It is theoretically grounded in general equilibrium theory, where rising output is the evidence of market efficiency and, by extension, welfare; and, following from that theory, embedded in the macroeconomic models treasuries, central banks, and financial institutions actually use to design policies and allocate resources (Pettini, 2025).
